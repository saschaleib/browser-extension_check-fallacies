Postscript

He thought leadership meant doing what was right, not what polled best — a view that made him reviled, until it made him respectable. Cheney was fine either way.

Former Vice President Dick Cheney is seen during an event in Washington, on May 12, 2014. | Win McNamee/Getty Images

Opinion by Matt Latimer

11/04/2025 05:45 PM EST

Matt Latimer is a former speechwriter for President George W. Bush. He is currently a co-partner in Javelin, a literary agency and communications firm.

Once upon a time there was this humorless ogre who terrorized villagers just to hear their screams, twirled an imaginary fiendish mustache as he stockpiled oil barrels in his living room, had a pet dragon named Halliburton, and was the source of all evil in the modern world. Then the ogre endorsed Kamala Harris, and presto! Dick Cheney was no longer the most despised man by the global social set.

Maybe it’s inconvenient as outlets do the ritual glowing remembrances of the former vice president, who died Monday at the age of 84, but I remember the other guy. The guy who for most of this century was vilified in movies (like Oliver Stone’s W, one of the most one-sided hit jobs in the history of Hollywood), countless bestselling books and drive-by assassinations on “Saturday Night Live.” How the Ivy League-trained writers of America had fun with Yale dropout Dick Cheney! The jokes weren’t always kind — they usually weren’t in fact — and were often personal and sometimes homophobic. Cheney’s daughter, Mary, who happened to be a lesbian, was much to her family’s chagrin a source of fascination by his many critics.

A typical joke after Cheney accidentally shot someone on a hunting trip: “There is a little discrepancy about what happened on this hunt, because Ann Armstrong, the woman who has this ranch, said there was no alcohol involved, and Dick Cheney said he had one beer. So apparently, Dick Cheney can’t keep his rifle, his story, or his daughter straight.”

Of course there were more, many more, centered on the Prince of Darkness. “According to the Washington Post, Vice President Dick Cheney is limping today because he injured his foot. Cheney said ‘If you think my foot looks bad, you should see the old lady I was kicking.’”

“Dick Cheney said he felt terrible about shooting a 78-year-old man, but on the bright side, it did give him a great idea about how to fix Social Security.”

Sure, some of that was rough — but the ogre deserved it, right? So did his family, didn’t they? Rep. Liz Cheney, his other daughter, of course remembers how she was treated, with jokes and derision, before she became a leading member of the Trump resistance. One comedian cracked on her ouster from Republican leadership, “She’s used to this. Her dad was a Dick, too.”

By the time his critics were done, Cheney had become so synonymous with the concept of evil that even Cheney himself gamely accepted it — once walking on stage to the “Imperial March” from Star Wars. Because why not? It can be liberating to be the bad guy everyone thinks you are. If you can never satisfy your critics, why even try? sThe Bush-Cheney era is remembered more fondly now at least compared to the current Republican administration to be sure, but let’s not forget that the same pundit class that would give us the Obama tan suit scandal, But Her Emails, and Mitt Romney the misogynist skillfully turned Cheney and the rest of the Bush administration into Satanic caricature, and then memory holed it when it became useful to contrast the Republicans from “the good old days” with the ones currently in office. The truth is “Bush = Hitler” was not a rare phrase back then. Is it any wonder millions of people have tuned these pundits out, ironically, when they are most needed?

It was Cheney in particular who was The Man Everyone Hated. But that wasn’t always true.

In a sense, like many Americans with a touch of gray in their hair, I grew up with him. In the Gerald Ford era, he was the guy who picked up Donald Rumsfeld from the airport in a beat-up old Volkswagen when Rumsfeld was summoned by the new president to be chief of staff after Nixon left office in disgrace. Rumsfeld made his somber young aide one of the most powerful men in America by bringing him on board as Ford’s deputy chief of staff. He’d later lose the “deputy” part as he became a no-nonsense, trusted aide to the president while Ford fought challenges from Ronald Reagan and then Jimmy Carter. By serving Ford and helping right the nation in a turbulent era, Cheney helped the country exorcise the ghosts of Watergate.

In the first Bush presidency, he was the efficient, if taciturn, defense secretary who humbled Saddam Hussein and, along with General Colin Powell, executed a 100-hour military incursion to evict Iraq from Kuwait. By a near-perfect display of American military power and restraint, Cheney helped the country exorcise the ghosts of Vietnam.

He was the guy who just got things done — humbly, quietly, ruthlessly, without a lot of eye contact. If only he had stopped there, and then.

It was of course the second Bush presidency — and Iraq again — that haunted him for decades. He was asked to help the younger George Bush find a VP — and both men ended up deciding he was the right guy for the job. Many jokes arose from that one, too. “That search committee was a diligent little enterprise,” Cheney once remarked, “and before long we found our man.”

At first, Cheney’s reputation for sharp-edged competence encouraged the pundit class to dub him the power behind the throne, the Wizard of Oz, Emperor Palpatine, Voldemort — to his dumb protege, George W. Bush, who wasn’t supposed to have been elected in 2000. Neither assertion was remotely true, of course — Cheney wasn’t all-powerful, Bush wasn’t dumb or even slightly incapable — but oh well.

Gradually, as the second Iraq war soured, so did the public’s view of the man many in Washington decided was almost single-handedly responsible for it. It was oil-hungry Cheney who wanted Iraq, who lied to the country that Saddam Hussein harbored weapons of mass destruction as a pretext — even though he wasn’t the only one who believed this, far from it in fact, or made that very case to a watching world. (*cough* *cough* Colin Powell.) Cheney (and Rumsfeld) were no longer the whiz kids who saved the American presidency after Watergate — but cold, inept and stubborn bumblers who all but destroyed it. None of which is to say Cheney didn’t deserve criticism for the Iraq War or his conduct as vice president — we were not in fact greeted as “liberators” in Baghdad, and of course there was no WMD as he among others asserted — but the vitriol seemed to stick to him more than it did to others equally, and sometimes more, deserving.

It was this Cheney — the guy one Pulitzer Prize winning humor columnist proudly and continually compared to Satan — whom I encountered when I came to work as a speechwriter for his boss, President Bush.

Having no desire to be president himself, he could be an effective “bad cop” for the president. He truly didn’t seem to care what people were writing or saying about him, which allowed him to make decisions that were unpopular. He probably should have cared a little more. In one of his rare unguarded moments, he told a senator to “go fuck himself” — which he later called “sort of the best thing I ever did.” He wasn’t warm and fuzzy — though he was a doting father and husband. He didn’t try to impress anyone. And he wasn’t always self-aware.

I once drafted a speech for Bush when he conducted the annual pardoning of the White House turkey. Every year Americans were given an opportunity to name the two turkeys Bush would symbolically pardon. I wrote a joke for the president, that he asked Cheney if he had suggestions for what to name the turkeys. In the joke, the vice president says, “Lunch” and “Dinner.” The president thought that was funny, but wanted us to run the joke by Cheney first, in case it seemed mean. Cheney had no objection — he just didn’t get it. He didn’t have time for joking around. He was a serious person who did serious things. And if people didn’t like it, well, that was just too damn bad.

Once he was told that according to some polls, two-thirds of Americans opposed the conduct of the Iraq war. He responded, “So?” This was seen as a notorious gaffe — he didn’t care about Americans! — and he could have elaborated more on that point than he did. But maybe that was his superpower, the secret to achieving his desired ends. He felt that leadership was doing what he thought was right, not what polled best. That didn’t make him popular with the pundit class most of the time, until it did quite memorably in 2024, but he was just fine with that.
